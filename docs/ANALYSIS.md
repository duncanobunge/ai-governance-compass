# Sixteen Frameworks, One Incoherent Picture: What the Global AI Governance Patchwork Means for Multinational Deployers

*By Duncan Obunge*

## The premise

Ask most people what "AI regulation" means and they'll point to the EU AI Act. Ask a compliance officer at a multinational company and you'll get a longer, more anxious answer — one that has to account for the fact that "regulation" currently means at least four structurally different things depending on which government you're talking to, and that several of the world's largest economies don't have binding AI law at all.

This piece is built directly on a structured comparison of sixteen AI governance instruments — binding statutes, administrative regulations, international treaties, and non-binding strategies — spanning Europe, North America, East Asia, Southeast and South Asia, Latin America, Africa, the Middle East, and three multilateral bodies, evaluated across seven governance dimensions: risk classification, legal status, data governance, transparency, human oversight, documentation, and enforcement.¹ The full dataset, with primary-source citations, is public. What follows are the four findings from that comparison that matter most for anyone actually trying to operate across borders — not because they're the most surprising, but because they're the ones with real operational consequences.

## Finding 1: "Regulation" doesn't mean one thing

It's tempting to picture global AI governance as a single spectrum running from "strict" to "loose," with the EU AI Act at one end and voluntary guidance at the other. The comparison makes clear that this picture is wrong in a way that matters.

China governs generative AI not through a parliamentary statute but through binding administrative measures issued by its Cyberspace Administration — rules with the force of law, but issued and amended through a fundamentally different process than legislation, layered alongside separate provisions on algorithms and synthetic media. The EU and South Korea, by contrast, have full legislative AI statutes passed through ordinary lawmaking, complete with phased compliance deadlines and parliamentary amendment processes. The Council of Europe's Framework Convention on AI is a binding international treaty — a third category entirely, enforceable only against states that ratify it. And a cluster of instruments from the African Union, the UAE, the OECD, and UNESCO are strategic or aspirational: real, influential, often widely cited — but carrying no enforcement mechanism of their own.

The practical consequence: two frameworks can both be accurately described as "binding" and still require completely different compliance postures. A company that has mapped its EU AI Act obligations has not thereby learned anything about how to interact with a Chinese administrative filing requirement, even though both are legally enforceable. Treating "binding vs. voluntary" as the only axis that matters is a category error that leads to real compliance gaps.

## Finding 2: Major economies still don't have binding AI law — and some never will, on the timeline you'd expect

There's a common assumption in governance discourse that the world is converging toward comprehensive AI statutes, and that jurisdictions without one yet are simply a few years behind. The data doesn't support that as a universal pattern.

Canada's Artificial Intelligence and Data Act — widely covered for years as the country's forthcoming comprehensive AI law — died on the legislative order paper in January 2025 when Parliament was prorogued, and has not been reintroduced as of mid-2026. Brazil's AI bill was approved by its Senate in December 2024 and has sat in committee review in the Chamber of Deputies ever since, with no fixed timeline for a floor vote. India has no dedicated AI statute at all; its AI-specific guidance exists only as non-binding advisories from its IT ministry, with actual binding obligations arising solely from its general data protection law.

This matters because "in progress" gets treated as functionally equivalent to "coming soon" in a lot of governance commentary, when in practice these three cases represent years of stalled or absent movement, not imminent arrival. A company planning compliance investment on the assumption that AI-specific law is coming to Canada, Brazil, or India in a predictable window is planning against evidence that doesn't currently exist. The safer planning assumption, in all three cases, is that general-purpose data protection law is what actually governs AI deployment today, and will continue to for the foreseeable future.

## Finding 3: Enforcement severity spans orders of magnitude — and that's an arbitrage opportunity, not just a compliance cost

Put the enforcement provisions from these sixteen frameworks side by side and the range is stark. The EU AI Act carries turnover-based fines reaching into the tens of millions of euros for the most serious violations. Kenya's Data Protection Act imposes fines capped at a small fraction of company turnover. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework — despite being one of the most widely referenced frameworks in industry practice — carries no penalty at all; accountability is entirely self-directed. ISO/IEC 42001's only consequence for non-conformity is loss of certification, a reputational and commercial cost rather than a legal one.

This isn't just a curiosity about relative severity — it creates a real incentive structure. An organization optimizing purely for legal risk exposure has a rational reason to prioritize compliance with frameworks that carry statutory fines over frameworks that carry only reputational or certification consequences, even when the latter are widely regarded as best practice. That's a governance design problem: frameworks with real teeth end up disproportionately shaping actual behavior, regardless of which frameworks are substantively the most rigorous or well-designed. A company's actual AI governance practice, in other words, is likely to look more like a map of enforcement severity than a map of governance quality.

## Finding 4: The only binding global AI treaty has a built-in reach gap

The Council of Europe's Framework Convention on AI holds a genuinely unusual status: it's the first legally binding international treaty specifically on AI, and it was deliberately opened for signature beyond the Council of Europe's own membership. But a treaty's binding force only extends to states that ratify it. This is structurally different from how the EU AI Act achieves its global reach — the EU Act binds any company placing AI systems on the EU market, regardless of where that company is headquartered, through market-access leverage rather than treaty ratification.

The result is a counterintuitive asymmetry: a regional regulation with no claim to international authority in principle achieves broader practical reach than the one instrument explicitly designed to be a global, binding AI treaty. For a company evaluating where its actual compliance exposure comes from, "is this instrument binding" is the wrong question. The right question is "does this instrument reach me regardless of where I operate" — and on that question, market-access regulations like the EU AI Act currently outperform treaty-based ones, ratified or not.

## What this means in practice

Four implications follow directly from these findings, for anyone responsible for AI governance across more than one jurisdiction:

**Don't build to the "strictest" framework and assume broader coverage.** Because these instruments differ in mechanism, not just in strictness, meeting the EU AI Act's documentation requirements doesn't automatically satisfy China's algorithm-filing requirements or Kenya's data protection impact assessment obligations. Each needs to be mapped independently.

**Track legislative status, not just framework existence.** A pending bill is not a law. Compliance planning built on "Brazil will have an AI law soon" or "Canada's AIDA will pass eventually" is planning against an assumption, not a fact — and in both cases, the assumption has been wrong for a while now.

**Weight your governance investment by actual reach, not by formal bindingness.** A treaty that binds only ratifying states may carry less real-world weight than a non-binding-in-name regulation that reaches you through market access. Map your actual exposure, not the formal legal category of each instrument.

**Expect enforcement-driven, not rigor-driven, prioritization — and correct for it deliberately.** If your organization's AI governance practice is quietly shaped more by which frameworks carry fines than by which frameworks are substantively best-designed, that's worth noticing and correcting for, rather than treating as a neutral default.

## Implications for Africa and other low- and middle-income settings

Everything above is framed around multinational deployers deciding where to spend compliance effort. But the picture looks different — and in some ways more precarious — from the vantage point of Africa and other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which sit on the receiving end of frameworks built elsewhere.

**Governance-by-proxy is the default, not the exception.** Kenya's Data Protection Act is, on paper, the country's binding instrument most relevant to AI — but it governs AI only indirectly, through personal-data rules.² An AI system that doesn't process personal data (a predictive-maintenance model for a factory, an agricultural-yield forecasting tool trained on satellite imagery) falls outside its scope entirely, regardless of how consequential its decisions are. This is not a Kenya-specific gap: across most of the African continent, and across many LMICs more broadly, data protection law is the *only* binding instrument that touches AI at all, because dedicated AI statutes largely don't exist yet. The practical effect is a coverage pattern shaped by what data protection law happens to reach, not by what AI risk actually looks like on the ground.

**Continental strategy has no enforcement teeth, and that's a deliberate, structural choice, not an oversight.** The African Union's Continental AI Strategy, adopted in 2024, sets shared principles and encourages harmonization across its 55 member states — but it is explicitly non-binding, with implementation left entirely to individual countries.³ That's a reasonable design given the AU's mandate, but it means the "African approach to AI governance" that gets referenced in international commentary is, in practice, an aspiration with no mechanism compelling any member state to act on it. The real governance landscape across the continent is closer to 55 separate, unsynchronized experiments than a single coherent regime — and a company or researcher citing "Africa's AI strategy" as if it functions like the EU AI Act is describing something that doesn't yet exist in enforceable form.

**LMICs inherit compliance frameworks built for a different risk profile.** Where African and other LMIC governments have engaged with AI governance, it has often been by referencing or adapting frameworks designed for high-capacity regulators and high-resource compliance functions — the EU AI Act's tiered risk model, or the OECD's principles, both built primarily around the concerns of economies with mature AI industries and well-resourced enforcement bodies. That's not inherently wrong — good governance ideas travel — but it means the risk categories, documentation burdens, and enforcement assumptions baked into these templates may not map cleanly onto contexts with different deployment patterns (e.g., agriculture, informal-economy credit scoring, humanitarian-response tooling) or different regulatory capacity to enforce them even if adopted.

**The practical stakes are asymmetric.** A multinational company that under-complies with a weakly enforced LMIC framework faces limited legal consequence, precisely because enforcement capacity is often the scarcest resource in these jurisdictions — which is a very different exposure calculus than under-complying with the EU AI Act. That asymmetry creates a real risk: AI systems deployed into African and other LMIC markets can end up governed, in practice, by whichever framework the deploying company chooses to take seriously, rather than by the law of the place actually affected. This is precisely why closing the "governance-by-proxy" gap — building AI-specific instruments that don't route through data protection law as an accident of scope — matters more in these contexts, not less: it's the difference between a jurisdiction setting its own terms and inheriting whatever a foreign framework happens to imply.

**What would help, concretely.** Three things would meaningfully change this picture, based on the pattern above: dedicated AI-specific provisions (even narrow, sector-specific ones — health, finance, agriculture) rather than relying solely on data protection law's incidental reach; binding regional coordination mechanisms with real teeth, rather than voluntary continental strategy alone; and locally grounded risk taxonomies that reflect actual deployment patterns in these economies, rather than imported tiering schemes built for a different industrial base. None of these require waiting for a comprehensive AI statute — Kenya's existing DPIA mechanism, for instance, could be extended with AI-specific triggers without a new Act, and this is exactly the kind of concrete, incremental recommendation this dataset is built to support.

## A living comparison, not a snapshot

None of this is static. Frameworks get amended, bills die or pass, treaties get ratified. The comparison underlying this piece is maintained as a public, versioned dataset for exactly that reason — every claim here traces to a specific clause in primary regulatory text, and the dataset's change history is itself part of the evidence trail. If you're working on AI governance across borders and want to check a specific claim, correct one, or add a jurisdiction that isn't yet covered, the dataset and its methodology are open for exactly that.
